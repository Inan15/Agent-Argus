### 3.A THE ONE HALT — a story wrote a fence that a later story in its own epic was required to breach

**The mechanics, stated precisely.** Story 17.3 wrote `TC-ArgusAgent-PRECISION-001-146` part (2) as a
**filesystem existence** assertion:

```python
for prefix in SUCCESSOR_OUTPUT_PATHS:
    assert not (_REPO_ROOT / prefix).exists(), (
        f"{prefix!r} exists. This story commits S1's CODE and never its OUTPUT over a "
        f"corpus member (AC6.4); committing one scored row would make Story 17.4's "
        f"ancestry guard argue about a commit nobody planned."
    )
```

Story 17.4's **AC6.1** required its record to land under `SUCCESSOR_OUTPUT_PATHS[0]` and **nowhere
else**; its **AC9.6** stated that `-135`..`-146` **are not edited**. The two cannot both hold. It is
not a judgement call — the story's deliverable existing on disk *is* the condition the guard reds on.
Worse, `-147` (the epic's own binding ordering constraint) was blocked by the same contradiction:
`DN-17-4-4` requires `-147` to assert a **non-empty** offender population, which is only satisfiable
at or after the first successor-output commit — the commit that reds `-146`.

**The resolution.** The dev refused to resolve it alone. The human operator amended AC9.6 **narrowly**
on 2026-08-26, re-scoping `-146` part (2) from a filesystem check to a claim over **Story 17.3's own
`(17-3)`-tagged commit range**, citing the `DN-17-2-12` precedent, and recorded two rejected
alternatives so the decision is auditable. The re-scoped guard was then **driven RED** in a throwaway
repository under `tmp_path` — never against the shared object database — and the demonstration is
committed *inside* the guard so it re-runs on every suite run.

**The process question, and the answer this retrospective gives.**

> *A story wrote a fence that a later story in its own epic was required to breach, and nothing caught
> it until the code was written. What would have caught it at contexting time?*

⛔ **Not a better reading of the guard's name.** Story 17.4's §0.12 and AC4.6 did exactly this analysis
for `-139` and got it **right** — `-139` walks commits reachable from `f906d04`, this story's commits
are descendants, so `-139` stays green. The authors then generalised from `-139` to `-146` and were
wrong, because **`-146` part (2) is a filesystem assertion and filesystem existence is not scoped by
commit ancestry**. Reading harder would not have separated those two cases reliably; §0.2 even records
*"both ABSENT on disk at HEAD (re-checked)"* as a **premise**, without noticing that a frozen guard
mechanically enforces that premise and that this story is chartered to end it.

⛔ **What would have caught it is EXECUTION, not reading.** The contexting pass already runs the suite
to establish a green baseline. What it did not do — and what nothing in the loop does — is run the
suite against a **stub of the story's own declared deliverable**: create the declared output paths,
re-run, and record which committed guards go RED. That takes two commands, requires no judgement, and
would have surfaced `-146` at contexting, where the amendment is cheap, instead of after the
measurement, where it cost a HALT and an operator round-trip. **Filed as `AI-E17-2`.**

⛔ **And the deeper class is that this is the SECOND time.** `DN-17-2-12` built the identical repair
one story earlier — `-144`'s git half was scoped to its own `(17-2)`-tagged commits *precisely because
the literal wholesale form would redden the moment a later story legitimately wrote inside the fenced
area* — and 17.2's code review judged it sound at the time. The insight existed inside this epic,
in a reviewed and passed story record, and **did not propagate to the next story's contexting.**
A fence whose truth expires is a **temporal claim** and must be written as a claim over its author's
own commit range. **Filed as `AI-E17-1`.**
